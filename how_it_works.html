01The problem this solves

Existing dark-web uptime monitors answer one question: is this site reachable right now, yes or no. That's a binary flag on a timer.

That's not enough for an analyst. "The site is down" doesn't tell you whether to expect a wave of vendor migration (exit scam), a law-enforcement press release (seizure), a temporary blip (DDoS attack), or nothing at all because it just moved addresses (migration). Four very different situations, one identical-looking symptom.

This kit watches a real .onion address over time and reads the shape of its behavior — not just whether it's up, but how it went down, how often, and what its content looked like — to guess which of those four situations is actually happening, and explain exactly why it thinks so.

02Abstract

The condensed version of the whole project, one paragraph. Full text lives in ../abstract.txt — this is that, lightly trimmed.

Dark web marketplaces frequently go offline, but existing monitors treat this as a binary signal — reachable or not — without explaining why. This project reframes monitoring as a temporal pattern-classification problem: four candidate causes (exit scam, law-enforcement seizure, DDoS-induced flapping, voluntary migration) are distinguished by quantifiable features — onset abruptness via CUSUM changepoint detection, downtime duration, flapping frequency, mirror-domain overlap — plus a passively-observable content signal (bounded hash-and-pattern-match against known seizure-banner phrasing, since a takedown page often still returns HTTP 200). An interpretable, rule-based classifier assigns a likely cause, attaches a real numeric confidence score, and reports the specific evidence that drove the decision. Validated 4/4 on synthetic timelines built to match each category, and field-tested through a passive Tor-based monitor collecting real onion-service history through the identical code. Positioned deliberately as a zero-cost, ethically passive corroboration signal — not a replacement for commercial platforms that already fuse uptime with blockchain and forum data — with two stated, sourced limitations bracketing its reliability ceiling: blind to a covert takedown, foolable by a faked one.

03Related work — where this sits in the literature

Two studies are the closest prior art, and neither does what this does — but one of them directly challenges the assumption this project's signatures rest on. Full writeup: ../METHODOLOGY.md §9.

StudyWhat it usedWhat it found
Wang, Arief & Hernandez-Castro, "Dark Ending", ICISSP 2023 (Best Student Paper, University of Kent) Weekly scraped content snapshots from 6 markets through closure Their two LEA-takedown markets showed no flapping; their two exit-scam markets were preceded by DDoS — the inverse of the onset/flapping mapping this project's classifier encodes. ElBahrawy et al., "Collective dynamics of dark web marketplaces", Scientific Reports 10:18827 (2020) Blockchain transaction data — 133M Bitcoin transactions, 31 markets, 24 real closures classified Police raid / exit scam / voluntary closure, from financial flows — not uptime.
Why this matters, stated plainly
The nearest comparable study that actually checked found the assumed signatures can collide in practice. That's not hidden here — it's why this project's own real-data validation (METHODOLOGY.md §10–11) matters more than the synthetic 4/4, and why confidence scores exist instead of bare labels.

04What's actually novel here

Narrow and real, not a breakthrough — stated at that size on purpose.

The core claim: to our knowledge, no prior academic work classifies outage cause from temporal reachability signatures alone — no content scraping, no blockchain access. Both studies above use richer data sources than this project does. That gap is genuine but narrow, and it cuts both ways: this project also inherits no ground-truth validation from either of them.

Below that headline claim, five specific engineering choices are each individually well-established techniques, applied in combination to this problem for what appears to be the first time here:

  • CUSUM changepoint detection (Page, 1954) in place of a fixed slope threshold for onset abruptness — adapts to a signal's own noise level instead of a hand-tuned constant.
  • SimHash near-duplicate fingerprinting for content-drift detection — tolerates routine noise (a changed login token) while still catching a real content swap, instead of an exact hash that flips on any single byte.
  • A small, from-scratch, fully auditable classifier as a secondary content signal, deliberately not an embedding/deep model — zero new dependencies, every prediction shows the specific words that drove it.
  • Confidence as evidence fusion — a real combined score from independent signals, instead of a hardcoded label per decision branch.
  • A reference-panel confound control for Tor's own network-wide DDoS periods — distinguishes a target-specific attack from a bad day for the whole network.
The honest positioning (see §9 of METHODOLOGY.md)
This is not pitched as competing with commercial/OSINT platforms that already fuse uptime with blockchain and forum data — they're faster and richer where funded. The defensible niche: a zero-cost passive corroboration signal for parties without that access, fast deconfliction for anyone not read into a specific operation, and — the strongest forward direction — generalizing beyond marketplaces to infrastructure like ransomware leak sites, where content/blockchain signals may not exist at all.

The decision procedure — fixed priority order

PriorityConditionCategory
1Seizure banner observed (direct content evidence)le_seizure
2Declared-pair mirror overlapmigration
3No permanent outage, ≥4 transitionsddos_attack

Every branch is a plain if/elif chain, deliberately — not a trained decision tree. With a handful of labeled examples to learn from, a trained tree would just memorize; an explicit rule set is equally powerful at this data scale and fully auditable, which matters more for a tool an analyst has to trust.

06The big picture

Two programs you actually run, one file that connects them.

Two independent programs, connected only by the CSV file. You run monitor.py continuously in one terminal; you run analyze_real_log.py whenever you want a read-out, in a separate terminal, as many times as you like.
The one thing to understand first
monitor.py and analyze_real_log.py never talk to each other directly. monitor.py only ever appends to uptime_log.csv. analyze_real_log.py only ever reads it. That's deliberate — you can stop and restart either one independently, and re-run the analysis as many times as you want without touching the monitor at all.

07What each file does

7 files. Two you actually run. Four are support code the other two import. One is a template you copy.

▶ monitor.py
you run this

Collects data. Connects to your .onion target through Tor once per interval (hourly by default), records whether it responded and how fast, then runs four lightweight content checks (via signal_utils.py and content_classifier.py — see §08) and saves all of it, results only, as one new row in uptime_log.csv. This is the only file that talks to the network, and the only one that ever computes a content-type guess — analyze_real_log.py just reads what it already decided.

▶ analyze_real_log.py
you run this

Reads the results. Loads the whole CSV, turns raw per-check rows into an hourly up/down timeline, measures its shape (how it dropped, how often it flapped, whether a declared mirror took over), and asks the classifier for a verdict. Prints a plain-English report. Never touches the network.

⚙ signal_utils.py
support code

Content fingerprinting. Used at both stages, unlike content_classifier.py: monitor.py calls it at collection time to hash the page (two ways — exact and "fuzzy"/tolerant of small changes) and check the text against known seizure-banner phrasing; analyze_real_log.py calls it again at analysis time to compare those already-saved fuzzy fingerprints and detect content drift. No network code, no stored state — pure functions either way.

⚙ content_classifier.py
support code

Guesses the page type. Also used by monitor.py at collection time: a small trained model that buckets the page into "looks like a seizure notice / error page / rebrand notice / normal page" — catching wording the exact-match check in signal_utils.py has never seen before.

⚙ downtime_cause_classifier.py
support code

The core decision logic. Used by analyze_real_log.py at analysis time: given the shape measurements, decides which of the four causes fits best, in a fixed priority order (the exact table is in §05).

⚙ confidence_scoring.py
support code

How sure to be. Called from two different places (see §08's second diagram): once inside downtime_cause_classifier.py's decision itself, and once more directly by analyze_real_log.py afterward to fold in evidence only visible across multiple monitored sites. Turns evidence into a real 0–1 score, not a hand-picked label.

📝 targets_local.example.py
you copy this

Template only. Copy it to targets_local.py and put your real, authorized .onion address in it. monitor.py automatically picks it up if it exists. Never committed to git — real addresses stay off the record.

08Inside the verdict — two separate phases

This is the part easiest to get wrong, so it's worth being precise: content_classifier.py runs once, at collection time, inside monitor.py — never again later. analyze_real_log.py only ever reads the result it already saved. Two diagrams, because two different programs run them at two different times.

Phase 1 — collection time (runs inside monitor.py, once per check)

These two files' answers become plain columns in the CSV — content_type_guess, content_type_confidence, content_sha256, etc. Nothing here runs again later; the CSV is the permanent record of what they decided in the moment.

Phase 2 — analysis time (runs inside analyze_real_log.py, whenever you ask for a read-out)

confidence_scoring.py appears twice on purpose: once inside classify() (using only this one site's own evidence, every time), and — only for the ddos_attack and ambiguous exit_scam_or_le_seizure verdicts specifically, not every verdict — once more directly by analyze_real_log.py afterward, to fold in evidence classify() has no way to see on its own, like whether OTHER monitored sites were also degraded at the same time.

10Three extra checks beyond the core verdict

The four-cause verdict is the headline, but analyze_real_log.py prints more than that when the evidence is there. Worth knowing about before you see them in real output and wonder where they came from.

1. Content-drift detection — catches wording the exact-match list has never seen

signal_utils.py compares each check's "fuzzy" fingerprint to the previous one. A big drop in similarity means the page changed by more than routine noise (a login token, a live counter) — and when it does, the classifier's stored guess for that specific check gets attached to the report, so you learn what kind of change it looked like, not just that something changed.

2. The ambiguous-case HINT — a second opinion when the primary signal is silent

When the verdict comes back exit_scam_or_le_seizure (the honest "can't tell" case), analyze_real_log.py separately checks whether the content classifier ever flagged seizure_banner at high confidence on an earlier check — even though the exact-match banner list missed it. If so, that gets appended as a HINT, pointing at the specific timestamp worth a manual look. It never changes the verdict itself, only adds a pointer.

Both of the above, together, in real captured output
predicted cause: exit_scam_or_le_seizure  [confidence: low, score: 0.27]
reason: ...the known ambiguous case... - HINT: no exact banner phrase matched,
but the content classifier guessed seizure_banner at 100% confidence on the
check at 2026-07-03T07:00:00+00:00 (driven by: has, permanently, taken,
criminal, evidence) - worth a manual look, not confirmed
content stability: 84% avg check-to-check similarity (1 significant change event(s) detected)
  - content changed significantly at 2026-07-03 07:00:00+00:00 (similarity dropped to 47%)
    - looks like: seizure_banner (100%, driven by: has, permanently, taken, criminal, evidence)
This is a real run's output (not hand-typed) against a page that said "infrastructure taken into custody following a joint international investigation" — wording that matches zero patterns in signal_utils.py's exact list, but the classifier still recognized it, flagged it, and pointed straight at the check that mattered.

3. Reference-panel and declared-mirror checks — only relevant with 2+ monitored sites

Two optional, more advanced checks only activate if you're watching more than one site at once — not required for a single target, but good to know they exist:

  • Reference panel (REFERENCE_SITE_NAMES in analyze_real_log.py): if a ddos_attack verdict's target was flapping at the same time an unrelated reference site was also down, confidence gets discounted — it might be a bad Tor day for everyone, not an attack on your target specifically.
  • Declared mirror pairs (KNOWN_MIRROR_PAIRS in analyze_real_log.py): migration detection only fires for pairs you explicitly name as suspected mirrors of each other — never guessed automatically, to avoid falsely linking two unrelated sites that just happened to both be up.

11A worked example

What actually lands in the CSV, and what comes back out — real captured output, not a hand-typed approximation.

One row monitor.py writes (simplified)

timestamp_utc: 2026-08-24T09:00:00+00:00
site_name:     my_target
status:        up
response_ms:   640
content_state: normal
content_type_guess: normal_marketplace  (94% confidence)

Hours later, the same site goes dark permanently. A later check might log:

status: down
error_class: connection_error

What analyze_real_log.py actually printed, for that exact scenario

my_target  (72h observed, 0h with no check data, forward-filled)
  predicted cause: exit_scam_or_le_seizure  [confidence: low, score: 0.18]
  reason: activity stayed flat/stable right up to an instantaneous, permanent
  outage, and no seizure banner was ever observed - the known ambiguous case:
  could be a seizure with no banner shown, or an exit scam with an instant
  rather than gradual cutoff (needs a signal beyond uptime pings to resolve)

That "ambiguous, low confidence" result isn't a failure — it's the system correctly refusing to guess when the evidence genuinely doesn't distinguish an exit scam from an unbannered seizure. If a takedown banner had been served instead, the verdict would be le_seizure at high confidence (score 0.97 in practice), with the exact matched phrase quoted in the reason. And if the classifier had spotted seizure-like wording the exact-match list missed, you'd see the HINT from §10 attached to this same line.

confidence_scoring.py — turning evidence into a real number

>>> from confidence_scoring import ddos_confidence, score_to_label

>>> score, factors = ddos_confidence(n_transitions=50)   # no reference-panel data
>>> score, score_to_label(score)
(1.0, 'high')

>>> score, factors = ddos_confidence(n_transitions=50, network_event_down_frac=0.42)

13Limitations — the honest ceiling

Two real, sourced, public-record cases bracket what this tool cannot do — from opposite directions. Full writeup: ../METHODOLOGY.md §10–11.

CaseWhat happenedWhat it means for this tool
Hansa, 2017
(Operation Bayonet)		 Dutch police secretly seized and operated Hansa for nearly a month before its own eventual shutdown. Public reporting describes "perfect, uninterrupted uptime" throughout. A reachability-only classifier watching Hansa during that window would report stable_no_outage — correctly, by its own signal, since there was no outage to observe. Blind to a covert takedown.
ALPHV/BlackCat, March 2024 The ransomware group's own operators posted a recycled copy of a real December 2023 law-enforcement seizure banner as cover for an exit scam (kept a ~$22M ransom instead of paying their affiliate). Europol, DOJ, and the UK NCA all denied involvement. Run through this project's logic, that fake banner matches le_seizure at high confidence — incorrectly. Nothing here verifies a banner's authenticity, only its text. Foolable by a fake overt one.

Together: this tool can be blind to a real covert seizure, and fooled by a fake overt one. Stated here, and on the poster, as the two headline limitations — not because they're the only ones, but because they're the sharpest, most concrete illustration of where the reliability ceiling actually sits.

14How to run it

Full command list lives in QUICKSTART.txt in this same folder — this is the same steps with a bit more of the "why" attached.

1
One-time setup — creates an isolated Python environment and installs the three packages this kit needs: 
cd ~/Persistent/quickstart_kit
python3 -m venv venv
source venv/bin/activate
pip install requests pysocks pandas
2
Tell it what to watch — copy the template and put your real, authorized address in it: 
cp targets_local.example.py targets_local.py
# then edit targets_local.py
3
Smoke test — one check, confirms Tor + the code work together: 
python3 monitor.py --once
4
Start real collection — leave this running in its own terminal: 
python3 monitor.py --loop --interval 3600
5
Whenever you want a read-out (in a second terminal, after activating the same venv) — safe to re-run as often as you like, it only reads: 
python3 analyze_real_log.py
The mental model to keep
One terminal is always collecting (step 4, left running for hours/days). A second terminal is where you occasionally ask "what do we know so far?" (step 5). They never block each other.

15Appendix: glossary of terms

Every term used earlier in this document, in one place — grouped by domain concept vs. project-specific concept, not in citation order.

Dark web / Tor concepts

Tor network
"The Onion Router" — an anonymity network that routes traffic through several relays so neither end can easily identify the other. .onion addresses only exist on this network.
.onion address network
A special address reachable only through Tor, hosting a "hidden service" whose real server location isn't exposed to visitors.
Onion / hidden service network
A website or service hosted at a .onion address — what this kit monitors.
Dark web marketplace domain
An online marketplace reachable only via Tor (or similar), the kind of target this project was originally framed around, before generalizing (§04).
Reachability domain
The most basic possible signal about a target: did it respond at all, yes or no. What every "binary" uptime monitor stops at — the whole premise of this project is that reachability alone isn't enough.
Exit scam cause
Market administrators voluntarily disable withdrawals and disappear with funds held in escrow — no external interference, a deliberate disappearance.
(Law enforcement) seizure cause
A government agency takes control of a service's infrastructure, ending its operation — or, per §13's ALPHV case, sometimes only claiming to.
Seizure banner signal
A notice page a seized service sometimes displays announcing the takedown — the specific text signal_utils.py's exact-match check looks for, and what content_classifier.py tries to recognize even when the exact wording is new (§04, §12 case 2).
DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) cause
A flood of traffic aimed at making a service unreachable — typically temporary, producing the "flapping" signature below.
Flapping signal
Repeatedly going up and down in quick succession — the DDoS signature, measured as num_transitions.
Migration cause
A market voluntarily moves to a new address (a "mirror"), the original being retired — detected only for explicitly declared candidate pairs (see below), never guessed automatically.

This project's own technical terms

Onset abruptness feature
How suddenly a service went from normal to fully down — "sudden" (no warning) vs. "gradual" (a decline beforehand), measured via CUSUM below.
CUSUM changepoint detection method
A statistical technique (Page, 1954) that flags when a signal's trend shifts, by accumulating deviations from a baseline until they cross a threshold — used here instead of a fixed slope cutoff so the decision adapts to the signal's own noise level (§05).
Exact hash signal
A SHA-256 fingerprint that changes completely if even one byte of the content changes — good for "is this byte-identical to before," useless for "did it change a little or a lot."
SimHash / fuzzy fingerprint signal
A near-duplicate-detection hash where similar content produces similar (not identical) fingerprints, so a changed login token doesn't look like a totally different page the way an exact hash would (§10's function reference, §12's example).
Content drift signal
A significant content change between two checks, detected by comparing SimHash fingerprints — independent of whether an exact-match banner was ever found (§10).
Confidence score output
A real 0.0–1.0 number for how strong the evidence behind a verdict is, computed by combining signals — not a hand-picked "high/moderate/low" label (§08).
Evidence fusion method
Combining multiple independent pieces of evidence (each pushing "for" or "against" a hypothesis) into one confidence score, instead of relying on a single feature and a fixed threshold.
HINT output
A note appended to an otherwise-ambiguous verdict when the content classifier independently suspected a seizure banner even though the exact-match check found nothing — never changes the verdict itself, only flags it as worth a manual look (§10).
Reference panel method
Known-stable sites monitored alongside your real target, used to tell whether flapping reflects a network-wide problem rather than an attack on your target specifically (§10).
Declared mirror pair method
Two site labels you explicitly tell the tool are suspected to be related — required before migration detection will ever fire for them; never inferred automatically (§10).
Corroborating signal concept
Evidence that supports or adjusts a verdict without being its primary basis — can raise or lower confidence, never overrides the primary decision.
Forward-filled (gap) reporting
For hours with no check data at all (monitor wasn't running), the last known status is carried forward, since there's no evidence anything changed — the hour count for this is always reported separately, so gaps are never silently hidden.
Ambiguous case verdict
Shorthand for exit_scam_or_le_seizure — the honest "can't tell" verdict, reported instead of a forced guess when the evidence genuinely doesn't distinguish an exit scam from an unbannered seizure.
